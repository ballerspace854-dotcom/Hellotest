RULE 1: WRITE LIKE A 10-YEAR-OLD COULD READ IT
────────────────────────
- 5th-grade reading level. Short words. Short lines.
- Headline: 3 to 8 words. Support line: 12 words or less.
- No jargon. Never use: barrier, emollient, formulation, dermatological, hydration matrix, nourish, holistic, clinically, revolutionary, game-changer.
- Say it like a tired parent would say it to a friend. Example: not "replenishes moisture," but "puts water back in dry skin."

────────────────────────
RULE 2: STATE FACTS, NOT OPINIONS
────────────────────────
People argue with opinions ("the best cream ever"). They don't argue with facts. Show a true fact and let the parent decide for themselves.
Use ONLY these facts:
- Afterwell has sea moss, aloe vera and oats.
- No steroids. No added fragrance.
- 4 fl oz jar.
- Contains oats, so patch test first.
- Doctors say: put cream on skin that is still a little damp after a bath.
- Doctors say: scratching helps for a second, then makes the itch worse.
- Hot water and indoor heating dry skin out.
- Kids' skin doctors say to pick fragrance-free products.
- "Unscented" can still have a scent added to cover smells. "Fragrance-free" is the one to look for.
- A UK eczema charity says that for some people with sensory issues, a gentle touch can feel like fire.
- A common doctor tip: the best cream is the one your child will actually let you put on.
Do not invent numbers, studies, percentages, review counts or timelines.

────────────────────────
RULE 3: OPEN A LOOP THEY HAVE TO CLOSE
────────────────────────
Every headline puts two things together that don't seem to fit. That makes the parent stop and work it out. Don't answer the puzzle in the headline. The support line or the picture answers half; the landing page answers the rest.
Examples of the feeling we want:
- "Thicker cream. Drier skin." (How can more cream mean drier skin? Because it gets wiped off.)
- "The cream worked. The arm didn't." (It never stayed on.)
- "Unscented doesn't mean no smell."

────────────────────────
RULE 4: SAY LESS, MEAN MORE
────────────────────────
The words should hint at a bigger story the parent fills in from their own life: the bedtime fight, the stares, the night of scratching. Don't spell out the feeling ("you feel so sad and guilty"). Show a small true moment and let the parent feel it.
Example: a jar sitting on the nightstand next to a pair of socks pulled over a child's hands. No words about sadness needed.

────────────────────────
RULE 5: TALK ABOUT THE PARENT'S LIFE, NOT THE PRODUCT
────────────────────────
At least 2 of every 3 ads must start with the parent's moment, not the jar. The jar shows up as the answer, not the hero.
Facts about the product only appear to back up a headline.

────────────────────────
RULE 6: KEEP THE FEELING CALM
────────────────────────
Most ads should feel calm and caring, or a little worried. None should feel scary or dramatic. No guilt-tripping the parent. The parent is never the bad guy. The bad guy is the greasy cream, the hidden scent, the dry winter air, the itch.

────────────────────────
RULE 7: BIG SWINGS, NOT SMALL CHANGES
────────────────────────
Meta treats ads that look alike as one ad. So every ad must change at least TWO of these: the parent's situation, the photo or scene, the layout style, the hook type. Never reuse a photo with a new headline.

────────────────────────
RULE 8: DESIGN THE AD AS A PHONE SCREEN
────────────────────────
Design at 1080 × 1350. Check that the headline reads in one second at thumbnail size. Big bold headline, one support line, small AFTERWELL logo, jar always visible.

────────────────────────
THE 30 NEW ADS
────────────────────────
Six parent situations (big swings across them):
P1 · Their kid wipes cream off right away.
P2 · They wait until the kid is asleep to put cream on.
P3 · They've tried every cream in the store.
P4 · They want something for every day besides steroid cream.
P5 · They're scared to try a new cream after a bad reaction.
P6 · They read every label.

Hooks below are starting points. Make them better if you can, but keep the rules.

BEFORE EACH IMAGE, CHECK:
────────────────────────
✔ Could a 10-year-old read the headline in one second?
✔ Is every claim one of the facts in Rule 2?
✔ Does the headline open a puzzle the parent wants to solve?
✔ Does it start with the parent's life (unless it's an us vs. them or ingredient ad)?
✔ Is the jar exact, the eczema clearly visible, and is there no blood?
✔ Does it look different from the last ad (scene + layout + hook type)?
